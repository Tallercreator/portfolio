## Context and problem

We needed a goal system four levels deep: company → quarter → team → employee. Goals break down as you go down, and progress adds back up as you go up.

Three things made it harder. A single goal can have several owners. Each person's contribution is set by its own weight. And a goal moves through approval stages, with different things possible at each one.

Drawing a four-level tree isn't hard. Making it clear where you are in that tree is: someone opens their own goal and can't see what it's part of, and a manager can't tell which level everything stalled at.

## Inputs

The project didn't exist yet. For anyone to start building it, the business had to see the future product and sign off the budget. There wasn't much time to prepare.

## The decision

<Note kind="fork">
Show the business static mockups, or build a working concept. The first is cheaper and faster, and time was short.

I went with the second. A four-level tree doesn't come across in pictures — you have to handle it: open a branch, watch progress recalculate when a goal has several owners, see what happens moving between approval stages. With static screens all of that is explained by the person standing next to them, not by the product. At a budget review those are two different things.
</Note>

## Solution

I built a clickable HTML prototype and ran the logic on it: how a goal breaks down, how progress is calculated when there are several owners, what happens on the transitions between approval stages.

The side benefit was worth as much as the main one. The holes in the logic surfaced on the prototype — which is to say, before anyone had paid for that logic.

The prototype covered more than the browsing screens — it included the forms: goal type, unit of measurement, target, participation weight, period, assignee. At a budget review that counts for more than a pretty picture: it shows the system was thought through down to the fields, not down to the cover.

## Outcome and reflection

The concept was presented to the business and the development budget was approved.

I didn't take the project forward myself. My load on other modules was high enough that adding one more would have meant doing all of them worse, so I handed it to another designer. There was something to hand over: by that point the prototype and the logic diagrams existed on their own, not only in my head.
